#### Don’t Let Ideas Fly Away

Some of my best ideas arrive when I least expect them — suddenly, gently, like a whisper in a quiet room. The shower is a classic example. There’s no phone. No music. Just warm water, white tiles, and the rare silence that invites clarity. In that stillness, a thought will float by — sharp, elegant, promising. And just as quickly, it’s gone.

We move too fast. We live in loops of tasks and tabs and timelines. Even when we stop working, we don’t really stop thinking — just scrolling, consuming, reacting. It’s motion without meaning. In that blur, we lose things: small sparks, half-formed sentences, fleeting insights. We trade reflection for reaction.

What I’ve come to realize is this: ideas rarely shout. They don’t demand our attention. They wait quietly, appearing only when there’s space for them to emerge. That space — true stillness — is rare. But it’s essential. Stillness, as it turns out, is not laziness. It’s the birthplace of clarity.

### A Story: The Shower Fix

I remember wrestling with a specific problem for weeks. I wanted to share a Postman collection with my team — easily, versioned, and free of charge. But there was no perfect solution. Postman’s free tier didn’t offer team collaboration the way I needed it, and I wasn’t comfortable storing everything in their cloud. Every time I revisited the issue, I hit the same wall. It stayed with me, lingering in the background while I focused on other things.

Then, one evening in the shower, after a long day of work, it hit me.

Of course. I could use a Visual Studio Code extension called **REST Client**. With it, I could write the same requests directly into `.http` files and version-control them via GitHub. The team could simply clone the repo and run the requests without ever needing Postman at all. Simple, elegant, and most importantly—ours.

That moment was a spark. It solved a problem that had been quietly bothering me for weeks. And it happened only because I gave it space. That’s the magic of those quiet moments. They give room for ideas to breathe — and sometimes, to land.

### And a Loss

But not every idea makes it.

I’ve had plenty slip through my fingers. A solution I thought of during a walk, a sentence that arrived as I was drifting off to sleep. I told myself I’d remember. I didn’t. And later, all I was left with was the echo of something good. Not the thing itself — just the feeling that it was once there.

For quite some time, I stopped writing down my ideas. I thought I didn’t need to. I was focused, efficient, checking boxes. But slowly, something started to shift. I began to feel disconnected from my work. I was moving fast, but the meaning was fading. The creativity that once fueled me started to dry up. It didn’t happen overnight — but it happened.

That’s why I’m back to writing them down.

### When Ideas Slip Too Often

There’s something subtle — but dangerous — about losing too many ideas. Not just once, but repeatedly. At first, it’s frustrating. Then it becomes a pattern. And slowly, without noticing, that pattern begins to whisper a lie: _maybe the ideas weren’t that good to begin with_.

That’s how it begins. You stop trusting your instincts. You begin to question whether you’re really creative at all. You think, _if it mattered, I would’ve remembered it_. But that’s not how ideas work. They’re not always loud or persistent. They’re fragile, fleeting. They need you to care enough to catch them.

The more I let ideas drift away, the more disconnected I felt — not just from my work, but from myself. I wasn’t just losing thoughts; I was losing the thread that made the work mine. The insights, the sparks, the strange connections — those are the fingerprints of creativity. Without them, everything started to feel generic. Like I was executing tasks, not creating anything meaningful.

And that’s when I realized the cost of not listening. I wasn’t just losing ideas. I was losing a part of my creative identity.

### Systems of Listening

Now, when an idea comes, I honor it.

Obsidian is my main space. Sometimes it’s a voice note, other times a sticky note. I don’t wait for the perfect app or the perfect sentence. I just capture it — quick, raw, unfinished. Because once it’s written, it’s safe. It can be returned to, developed, or discarded. But it’s no longer lost.

Not every idea is brilliant. That’s not the point. They’re signals. Little reminders of what I care about, what I’m drawn to, what still moves me. And when I show up for them, more of them seem to show up for me.

### Ideas Are Visitors

The more I treat ideas like guests, the more I understand their nature. They don’t knock loudly. They pass by windows and cracks in the door, hoping someone notices. They won’t wait until we’re done with our emails. They won’t ask for permission.

But if we’re paying attention, if we’re open, they’ll leave something behind.

That’s the real message here. We can’t keep waiting for inspiration to shout louder. We have to slow down enough to hear it. And when it comes, we can’t assume it will stay. Make space. Stay open. Write it down.

Ideas don’t owe us anything. But if we’re present, they might just stay long enough to change something.
